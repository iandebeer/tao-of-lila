# Introduction: The category of Tao?

A Taoist might rightfully ask: "The what of Tao?", while an abstract mathematician will query: "The category of what?". There are many who would be more puzzled: " The what of what?"

There are many books on the Tao Te Ching. There are many books on the I Ching.
There are many books on category theory.

This book is interested in the space between them.

Its purpose is not to prove that ancient Chinese philosophy anticipated modern
mathematics. That would be both historically careless and philosophically too
small.

The more interesting question is this:

What becomes visible when one of humanity's oldest philosophies of change is
examined through one of modern mathematics' most powerful languages for
describing transformation?

Category theory begins not with isolated things, but with objects understood
through their relationships. The Tao Te Ching begins not with fixed doctrine,
but with the nameless movement from which named things arise. The I Ching begins
with change itself.

This book explores that shared attention to transformation.

The path through these pages now begins with an arrow: a crossing, a movement,
a relationship. We follow it through return and identity before asking how the
One and the many can be understood through their relationships. Form and
emptiness prepare the ground for ways that agree, translations between worlds,
and transformations of perspective.

Only then do we approach context, consequence and mutual fit. The Mother and
the ten thousand things bring us to the limits of treating the source as another
object. The question planted at the river—could a thing be known through its
relationships?—returns with Yoneda. The final chapter asks how that attention
to relationship might become unforced action: wu wei.

The method is experience, then image, then mathematics. A mathematical result
keeps its own precise meaning; the contemplative question it provokes remains
a question. The arrow, too, is a finger pointing at the moon.
