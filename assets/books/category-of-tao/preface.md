# Preface

The Category of Tao began as an unexpected companion to another project.

My original intention was to create a new interpretation of Lila, the ancient Indian game that represents the stages of spiritual development. Traditionally attributed to the Hindu tradition and often compared to the game of Snakes and Ladders, Lila is far more than a board game. It is a symbolic map of human consciousness and the journey of the soul. As this project developed, it became increasingly clear that it required a companion volume—one that explained the ideas that lay behind the board itself. This book is the result.

The journey that led to it, however, began much earlier.

For most of my professional life I worked in information technology, spanning a remarkable period of change. My career began before the Internet existed, when programs were literally entered on punch cards. Over the decades I witnessed—and participated in—the evolution of structured programming, object-oriented design, distributed systems, and eventually functional programming.

Towards the latter part of my career I encountered category theory. At first it appeared to be an abstract branch of mathematics, but the more I explored it, the more I realised that it provided something much deeper: a language for describing relationships, transformations and structure. Although I remained very much a student of the subject, its way of thinking profoundly influenced how I approached programming and, increasingly, how I approached the world itself.

Then everything changed.

A severe illness brought my professional career to an abrupt end. I spent six months in hospital, much of that time in intensive care. On two occasions I came close to death. I suffered a stroke that permanently affected my vision and underwent amputations that would forever change my relationship with my own body.

Recovery demanded far more than physical healing. It required the reconstruction of an identity.

It became evident that I would never return to the career I had known. The life I had carefully built over decades had ended, and another had to begin. Like many people who survive life-threatening illness, I found myself asking questions that had always been present but had suddenly become impossible to ignore. Why had I survived? What was I now being asked to become? How should one live after coming so close to death?

These questions led me back to spiritual traditions that had accompanied me throughout much of my life: Christian mysticism, Taoism, Zen Buddhism and Advaita Vedanta. At the same time, the practical realities of rehabilitation introduced me to Qigong, whose gentle movements became both physical therapy and contemplative practice.

At first these interests appeared unrelated. On one side stood mathematics, logic and computer science. On the other stood mysticism, philosophy and ancient wisdom traditions.

Yet I began to suspect that they were describing similar patterns.

Category theory had taught me to look less at individual objects than at the relationships between them. It asks not merely what things are, but how they transform into one another while preserving deeper structure. I began to wonder whether this way of thinking might also illuminate spiritual development—not by reducing ancient traditions to mathematics, but by offering a common language in which relationships, movement and transformation could be understood.

Around this time I discovered the game of Lila while simultaneously immersing myself in the Tao Te Ching and the I Ching. Quite unexpectedly, these streams of thought began to converge. The symbolic movement of Lila, the transformations embodied in the I Ching, the paradoxes of Taoism, and the structural insights of category theory all seemed to resonate with one another.

The result is neither a mathematical treatise nor a religious text. It is an exploration.

This book is an attempt to see whether concepts drawn from category theory can provide a fresh perspective on the perennial insights of Taoism and related contemplative traditions. It does not claim that Lao Tzu anticipated modern mathematics, nor that category theory proves spiritual truths. Rather, it asks whether the language of relationships and transformations can serve as a bridge between these worlds, allowing each to illuminate the other.

If this work succeeds, it will not be because it provides definitive answers. It will be because it encourages readers to see familiar ideas from an unfamiliar angle, discovering new connections where previously there appeared to be only separate disciplines.

Looking back, I can see that this book could never have been planned. It emerged from a convergence of illness and recovery, of programming and philosophy, of mathematics and mysticism, of endings and beginnings.

Sometimes the most meaningful journeys are not the ones we choose, but the ones that choose us.
There is another aspect of this work that I should acknowledge from the outset.

I do not write as an authority on category theory, nor as a scholar of Taoism, Zen, Advaita Vedanta, Christian mysticism, or the I Ching. Throughout this book I remain, in every sense, a student. If there is a qualification that I possess, it is curiosity.

One of the ideas that has long inspired me comes from the Zen teacher Shunryu Suzuki, who wrote that, “In the beginner’s mind there are many possibilities; in the expert’s mind there are few.” I have tried to approach this project in that spirit.

A beginner’s mind is not an uninformed mind. It is a mind that remains willing to be surprised. It is prepared to ask naïve questions, to notice unexpected correspondences, and to follow a line of thought without already knowing where it will lead.

That spirit has shaped the writing of this book in an unusual way. Traditionally, a preface is written after the book has been completed, when the author can look back and explain the journey that has already taken place. In this case, I find myself writing before the destination is known.

I know where the journey began. I do not yet know where it will end.

The ideas explored in these pages are not the execution of a carefully designed plan but the record of an unfolding investigation. There will undoubtedly be paths that prove unfruitful, assumptions that require revision, and discoveries that redirect the course of the work. I hope to remain open to all of these possibilities.

If this book has a method, it is simply this: to follow relationships wherever they appear, to allow mathematics and mysticism to converse without forcing agreement, and to resist the temptation of premature certainty.

Perhaps, when the final page has been written, I will discover that the book was about something quite different from what I imagine today. If so, that will not be a failure of the process, but its fulfilment.
