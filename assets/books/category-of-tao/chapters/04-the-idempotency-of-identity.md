# Chapter 4 — The Idempotency of Identity

## Wahe Guru

**As ek meer kon ruik,**  
sou my trane onophoudelik en vrylik vloei.

**As ek meer kon voel,**  
sou ek soos warm kool in die donker gloei.

**As ek meer kon hoor,**  
sou my vreugde dans, my bo die aarde laat sweef.

**As ek meer kon proe,**  
sou smake soos nektar aan my tong bly kleef.

**As ek meer kon sien,**  
sou my hart oop bars soos 'n ryp granaat.

**“Genoeg,” roep ek, “Genoeg. Ek is gevul in oormaat!”**

*Sabh Gobind hai, sabh Gobind hai, Gobind bin nahin koi.*

*All is the Divine; all is the Divine; there is nothing apart from the Divine.*

### For the English reader

If I could smell more,  
my tears would flow endlessly and freely.

If I could feel more,  
I would glow like warm coals in the darkness.

If I could hear more,  
my joy would dance and lift me above the earth.

If I could taste more,  
flavours like nectar would linger on my tongue.

If I could see more,  
my heart would burst open like a ripe pomegranate.

“Enough,” I cry, “Enough. I am filled beyond measure!”

*All is the Divine; all is the Divine; there is nothing apart from the Divine.*

---

The poem begins with **more**.

More smell. More touch. More sound. More taste. More sight.

The five senses through which the world reaches us seem to offer an inexhaustible succession of experience. There is always something else to smell, something else to touch, another sound to hear, another flavour to taste, another thing to see.

And yet the poem does not end with *more*.

It ends with:

**Genoeg.**

Enough.

Strangely, the cry of enough does not arise from deprivation or restraint. It arises from abundance:

**Ek is gevul in oormaat.**

I am filled beyond measure.

Nothing has been added at the moment at which *more* becomes *enough*. The world has not changed. Something in the relationship to the world has changed.

Perhaps this is where we should begin.

## When a hungry person arrives

Imagine that a hungry person arrives at your door.

You see the hunger. You have food.

You feed him.

There is almost no process to describe:

\[
\text{hunger}\longrightarrow\text{food}
\]

Perhaps even that is already saying too much. Hunger encounters compassion and the response arises naturally.

Now imagine the same hungry person arriving at an institution established specifically for the purpose of feeding hungry people.

He arrives.

His name is taken.

He completes a registration.

His eligibility is checked.

His identity is validated.

His need is classified.

Approval is obtained.

A meal allocation is made.

And finally:

\[
\text{arrival}
\rightarrow
\text{registration}
\rightarrow
\text{validation}
\rightarrow
\text{approval}
\rightarrow
\text{allocation}
\rightarrow
\text{food}.
\]

The person is fed in both cases.

Indeed, the second system may feed thousands where the first can feed only one. Process is not inherently wrong. Process allows action to be repeated, coordinated and extended beyond the spontaneous capacity of an individual.

But something has changed.

What was once an immediate relationship has become a sequence of operations.

And sequences have a peculiar tendency to grow.

If something goes wrong, we add a check.

If the check fails, we add a validation.

If validation can be abused, we add an approval.

If approval is inconsistent, we add a rule.

And if the rule fails:

we add another process.

There is always one more thing that can be done.

## Ritual

Chapter 38 of the *Tao Te Ching* describes a curious descent.

When Tao is lost, *De* remains.

When *De* is lost, benevolence appears.

When benevolence is lost, righteousness appears.

When righteousness is lost, there is **ritual — lǐ**.

Ritual is not meaningless. Like our process for feeding the hungry person, it attempts to preserve through form something that no longer arises spontaneously.

The bow remains after reverence has faded.

The rule remains after understanding has faded.

The procedure remains after relationship has faded.

Perhaps ritual is what happens when **being has to be replaced by doing**.

And once doing becomes the means by which we recover what has been lost, another temptation appears:

**more doing.**

## The world of more

There are therefore at least two ways in which *more* enters our lives.

We accumulate **things**:

\[
a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes\cdots
\]

And we accumulate **processes**:

\[
f\circ g\circ h\circ\cdots
\]

More having.

More doing.

Both seem to contain the same implicit promise:

> Something still lacking may be supplied by one further addition.

This suggests a first, deliberately tentative correspondence with the mathematical structure called a **monoid**.

A monoid consists of things that may be combined through an associative operation:

\[
(M,\otimes,I)
\]

so that

\[
(a\otimes b)\otimes c
=
a\otimes(b\otimes c),
\]

together with an identity \(I\):

\[
I\otimes a=a=a\otimes I.
\]

A monoid does not mean “endless accumulation.” Mathematics makes no such philosophical claim.

But the structure permits repeated composition:

\[
a
\rightarrow
a\otimes b
\rightarrow
a\otimes b\otimes c
\rightarrow
a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d
\rightarrow\cdots
\]

The operation itself contains no instruction saying:

**Enough.**

The possibility of further composition does not provide a criterion for sufficiency.

## Māyā

Perhaps this offers one way of approaching the Hindu idea of *Māyā*.

Not that the sensory world is simply false. The senses in *Wahe Guru* are not enemies. They are precisely the means through which the astonishing richness of existence becomes present.

The problem arises when multiplicity carries an implicit promise of completion.

One more experience.

One more possession.

One more achievement.

One more explanation.

One more procedure.

The assumption is:

\[
\text{more}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{nearer to enough}.
\]

But *more* has no natural destination called *enough*.

## Knowing enough

Chapter 44 of the *Tao Te Ching* asks a different question.

Name or self—which matters more?

Self or possessions—which is worth more?

Gain or loss—which brings greater harm?

And from these questions comes **知足 — zhī zú**:

**knowing enough.**

This is not necessarily an instruction to possess less.

It may be something considerably more radical.

Perhaps *enough* is not a quantity.

If so, no amount of addition can produce it.

This is the turn that occurs in *Wahe Guru*. The senses move repeatedly toward *meer* until suddenly:

**Genoeg.**

Nothing is added.

Yet everything changes.

## Idempotence

Mathematics has a word for an operation whose repetition adds nothing to its result.

**Idempotence.**

If \(p\) is idempotent:

\[
p\circ p=p.
\]

Once it has acted, acting again produces no further change.

If, purely as metaphor, \(E\) represents the recognition of sufficiency, then:

\[
E(E(x))=E(x).
\]

Enough cannot become more enough.

But something more surprising happens when we return to the monoid.

Every monoid possesses an identity \(I\):

\[
I\otimes x=x=x\otimes I.
\]

Now let \(x=I\):

\[
I\otimes I=I.
\]

The identity is necessarily idempotent.

The very structure that allows us to compose one thing with another, and another, and another, contains within itself an element to which its own repetition adds nothing.

This is not metaphor.

It is mathematically true.

The metaphor begins only when we ask what that mathematical truth might allow us to see.

## The Idempotency of Identity

Every identity is idempotent.

Not every idempotent is an identity.

That distinction may matter later.

For now there is something worth contemplating in the simple expression:

\[
\boxed{I\otimes I=I}
\]

Identity does not become **more itself** through repetition.

And perhaps this returns us to the final line of *Wahe Guru*:

*Sabh Gobind hai, sabh Gobind hai, Gobind bin nahin koi.*

All is the Divine; all is the Divine; there is nothing apart from the Divine.

If the Divine is understood as all that is, what could be added to it?

From where would the additional thing come?

The whole cannot become more whole.

Perhaps *genoeg* is therefore not the point at which we have finally accumulated sufficient pieces.

Perhaps it is the recognition that nothing was missing.

## Addition and subtraction

Chapter 48 of the *Tao Te Ching* approaches the question differently.

In the pursuit of learning, something is added each day.

In the pursuit of Tao, something is diminished each day.

One movement accumulates:

\[
x\rightarrow x+a\rightarrow x+a+b\rightarrow\cdots
\]

The other relinquishes.

Chapter 22 tells us that the empty may become full and that having much may lead to confusion.

Chapter 16 speaks of the myriad things returning to their root.

Chapter 38 takes us from Tao toward ritual and increasing contrivance.

Chapter 44 asks whether we know enough.

Chapter 48 asks what happens when, instead of adding, we begin to subtract.

These may not be separate teachings.

They may be different views of the same movement.

## What remains when predicates fall away

The movement reverses:

\[
I\otimes a\otimes b\otimes c
\rightarrow
I\otimes a\otimes b
\rightarrow
I\otimes a
\rightarrow
I.
\]

This is not a mathematical operation inherent in a monoid. It is a philosophical use of the image.

What are the things being removed?

Perhaps the predicates with which we construct ourselves:

**I am a writer.  
I am successful.  
I am old.  
I am learned.  
I am afraid.  
I am important.**

Remove what follows *I am*.

What remains?

**I am.**

And now identity begins to move beyond the merely mathematical toward the question of consciousness.

The Upanishadic method offers:

**neti neti**

Not this.

Not that.

Each attempted definition is relinquished.

\[
\text{I am this}
\rightarrow
\text{not this}
\rightarrow
\text{I am that}
\rightarrow
\text{not that}
\rightarrow
\cdots
\]

Yet the diminishing need not end in nihilistic nothingness.

Advaita offers another formulation:

**Sat–Chit–Ānanda**

Being.

Consciousness.

Bliss.

These are not three more possessions to be added to the perfected self. They point toward what cannot adequately be constructed through accumulation.

The mathematical identity does not possess consciousness. An identity morphism does not know itself.

But the mathematical image allows the contemplative question:

**What remains when everything by which I ordinarily identify myself is allowed to fall away?**


The destination has become visible. We are not there yet.

## Identity as non-interference

Category theory gives every object \(X\) an identity morphism:

\[
\mathrm{id}_X:X\rightarrow X.
\]

It participates fully in composition, but leaves its object unchanged:

\[
f\circ\mathrm{id}_X=f
\]

and

\[
\mathrm{id}_Y\circ f=f.
\]

And:

\[
\mathrm{id}_X\circ\mathrm{id}_X=\mathrm{id}_X.
\]

It would be a mistake to say that an identity morphism **is** *wu wei*. One is a mathematical construction; the other is a Taoist insight.

But they may be placed beside one another.

The identity morphism participates without imposing transformation.

It does not withdraw from the structure.

It simply adds nothing.

Perhaps there is a difference between **doing nothing** and **adding nothing**.

And perhaps *wu wei* has something to say about that difference.

For now these are correspondences, not conclusions:

\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
\text{Composition} &\longleftrightarrow& \text{more}\\
\text{Repeated composition} &\longleftrightarrow& \text{accumulation}\\
\text{Ritual} &\longleftrightarrow& \text{process}\\
\text{Idempotence} &\longleftrightarrow& \text{sufficiency}\\
\text{Identity} &\longleftrightarrow& \text{non-addition}\\
\mathrm{id}\circ\mathrm{id}=\mathrm{id}
&\longleftrightarrow&
\text{enough remains enough}
\end{array}
\]

Category theory does not prove the Tao.

The Tao does not predict category theory.

But sometimes two very different ways of looking reveal a similar shape.

And when that happens, perhaps the shape itself is worth contemplating.

**Genoeg.**

Perhaps that is enough for now.
