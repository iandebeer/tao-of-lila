# Chapter 2 — The Net of Heaven

### Nothing Is Lost

Near the end of Chapter 73 of the *Dao De Jing*, Laozi leaves us with one of his most mysterious images:

> 天網恢恢，疏而不失\
> *tiān wǎng huī huī, shū ér bù shī*

Heaven's net is vast.\
Its mesh is wide.\
Yet nothing is lost.

It is a peculiar net.

A fisherman's net catches because its mesh is small enough to prevent the fish from passing through. The tighter the weave, the more effective the net. Yet Laozi gives us the opposite image. Heaven's net is immense and loose. There are spaces everywhere.

And still nothing escapes.

Perhaps we misunderstand the net if we imagine that its purpose is to catch things.

Perhaps nothing slips through the net because nothing exists outside the net.

## The jewel and the thread

Centuries later, another great Asian tradition would imagine reality as a net.

In the Huayan tradition of Mahayana Buddhism we encounter the image generally known as **Indra's Net**.

Imagine an infinite net stretching in every direction. At every intersection hangs a jewel. Each jewel reflects every other jewel in the net.

But it does not stop there.

Each jewel reflects all the other jewels, which themselves reflect all the other jewels. Thus within one jewel appear the reflections of reflections of reflections, without any final boundary.

Pick up any jewel and, in some sense, you have picked up the whole net.

This is not because the universe has somehow been squeezed inside the jewel.

It is because the jewel cannot be separated from its relations.

This presents us with an interesting question.

What is fundamental:

the jewel,

or the threads that make the jewel an intersection?

We ordinarily imagine the jewels first.

There are things, we assume, and afterwards those things enter into relationships.

There is *me*.

There is *you*.

There is a tree.

There is a river.

There is the moon.

And then we draw lines between them.

Category theory invites us to look differently.

Begin with the arrows.

## Begin with the arrow

Suppose there are objects \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\).

Between them are morphisms:

\[
A \xrightarrow{f} B
\]

and

\[
B \xrightarrow{g} C.
\]

Category theory tells us that these relationships compose:

\[
A \xrightarrow{g\circ f} C.
\]

The path itself becomes a relationship.

This simple requirement has extraordinary consequences for our net.

The net does not require a thread directly joining every jewel to every other jewel.

It may indeed be *疏* — *shū* — sparse, loose, widely spaced.

If

\[
A\rightarrow B\rightarrow C\rightarrow D,
\]

then composition allows the relationships to travel through the net.

There need not be a primitive thread from \(A\) to \(D\). The path through \(B\) and \(C\) already relates them.

The mesh can therefore be open without the world falling through it.

Laozi's image suddenly acquires an unexpected mathematical resonance:

**the net may be sparse locally while remaining whole relationally.**

Nothing need be bound tightly to everything else.

There need be no central knot holding it together.

There need be no controller pulling the strings.

Relationship composes.

The net holds itself.

## Where, then, is the Dao?

There is a temptation here that must be resisted.

We might want to make the Dao the largest jewel.

Perhaps there are ten thousand jewels and then, somewhere beyond them, one supreme Jewel called Dao.

But this would make the Dao another thing.

Another object.

Another member of the category.

Laozi repeatedly refuses us this comfort.

The Dao that can be named is not the enduring Dao.

Dao does not stand among the ten thousand things as the greatest of the ten thousand and one things.

Nor should we simply identify Dao with a particular arrow joining everything together.

The Dao is not another jewel.

The Dao is not another thread.

If we are to use the categorical metaphor carefully, perhaps we should say only:

**Dao is that through which relation, transformation and composition are possible at all.**

And even that must immediately be released.

For the moment we define Dao, we have placed another jewel in the net and written *Dao* upon it.

Laozi smiles and the jewel disappears.

## The empty jewel

Indra's jewel takes us still deeper.

What would a jewel be if we removed everything it reflects?

Suppose we attempted to discover the jewel *in itself*.

We remove its relationship with this jewel.

Then another.

Then another.

We remove its position in the net.

We remove what enters it.

We remove what leaves it.

We remove every relation by which it can be distinguished.

What remains?

Nāgārjuna would warn us against expecting to discover some final substance hiding underneath all those relationships.

Things are empty of independent, self-existing essence.

But emptiness does not mean nothingness.

This is where the Afrikaans word **vol-ledig** becomes useful.

*Ledig*: empty.

*Vol*: full.

*Volledig*: complete.

The jewel is **ledig** of independent existence precisely because it is **vol** of relationship.

It is empty of itself.

Therefore it can reflect everything.

The jewel is **vol-ledig**.

## To know a thing by its relations

Much later in this book we shall encounter one of the most beautiful results in category theory: the Yoneda lemma.

We need not understand it yet.

For now we can approach it through the jewel.

Suppose we wish to understand an object \(A\).

Our instinct is to open it.

To look inside.

To ask:

**What is A made of?**

Category theory permits another question:

**How does A relate?**

What arrows arrive at \(A\)?

What arrows leave it?

How does every other object encounter it?

Instead of searching for some secret interior essence of \(A\), we examine its place in the web of morphisms.

And something remarkable happens.

In an important and mathematically precise sense, the pattern of those relationships is sufficient to determine the object within its category.

The jewel is revealed through what it reflects.

We shall return to this.

For now it is enough to leave a small jewel hanging in the reader's mind:

**perhaps a thing is not first a thing and afterwards related.**

Perhaps its relationships are inseparable from what it is.

## Nothing slips through

Now we can return to Heaven's net.

Why does nothing slip through?

The conventional image suggests an enormous cosmic net catching things.

But perhaps Heaven does not catch anything.

There is nothing to catch.

A thing can fall through a fisherman's net because the fish and the net exist independently. The fish approaches the net from elsewhere. It encounters the net. It either becomes trapped or passes through.

But what if the fish itself exists only within the relational world represented by the net?

Where would it fall?

What would *outside* mean?

An object utterly without relationship would have no arrows by which it could appear within our category.

It could not be approached.

It could not act upon anything.

Nothing could act upon it.

Within that categorical universe, there would be no means of distinguishing it from absence.

Thus nothing slips through the net, not because the holes are small, but because **there is nowhere outside the net to fall.**

Heaven's net need not imprison the ten thousand things.

It is not a cage.

It is their relatedness.

## Wu wei and the loose weave

This also gives us another way of approaching **wu wei**.

A tightly woven net requires construction.

Control.

Constraint.

Every gap must be closed.

Every possibility anticipated.

Every outcome managed.

This is the instinct of the administrator, the engineer and often the frightened mind.

If something might escape, tighten the net.

Add another rule.

Another measurement.

Another intervention.

But Heaven's net is *shū*.

Loose.

Open.

It does not micromanage the ten thousand things.

And yet:

\[
A\rightarrow B,\qquad B\rightarrow C
\]

already gives us

\[
A\rightarrow C.
\]

The world does not need every possible connection to be imposed upon it.

Relations compose.

Processes unfold.

Water finds the valley.

The seed becomes the tree.

The tree becomes fire.

The fire becomes ash.

The ash returns to earth.

No administrator is required to move each thing from square to square.

The net works precisely because it does not struggle to hold everything.

This is governance by wu wei.

This is action without forcing.

The net is loose enough for everything to move and complete enough that nothing is lost.

## No central jewel

Indra's Net offers one further challenge to our habitual thinking.

Where is its centre?

Choose a jewel.

From that jewel, every other jewel is reflected.

It appears to be the centre.

Move to another jewel.

Again the entire net appears.

That jewel now seems to be the centre.

Every jewel is a possible centre.

Therefore there is no privileged centre.

This is difficult for the ego.

The ego would like to be the jewel from which the net is viewed.

*I* see the world.

*I* encounter others.

*I* form relationships.

The world radiates outward from here.

But another jewel can make exactly the same claim.

And another.

And another.

Perhaps the self is not a sovereign jewel observing the net.

Perhaps what we call *self* is one temporary shimmering of the net at a particular intersection.

Not:

**first the self, then its relationships.**

But:

**relationship, from which something resembling a self temporarily appears.**

The jewel does not possess the reflections.

The reflections constitute the jewel.

## The ten thousand things

We can now return to Laozi's ten thousand things.

They arise.

They transform.

They return.

Category theory gives us objects and arrows.

Daoism gives us things and transformations.

Huayan gives us jewels and reflections.

Nāgārjuna removes the independent substance we keep trying to place beneath them.

Different traditions.

Different purposes.

Different histories.

We should not pretend that they secretly taught category theory thousands of years before mathematics discovered it.

They did not.

But sometimes a mathematical structure gives us another language with which to notice an ancient insight.

And sometimes an ancient image allows us to see what a mathematical abstraction means.

Heaven's net is vast.

Its weave is loose.

There is no central knot.

There is no supreme jewel.

There is no hand desperately holding the threads together.

There are simply the ten thousand things arising through relation, transformation and return.

One jewel reflects another.

One morphism composes with another.

One thing becomes another.

And perhaps what we have been calling *things* were never quite as separate as their names suggested.

The net catches nothing.

Nothing slips through.

The jewel contains nothing.

It reflects everything.

It is empty.

It is full.

It is **vol-ledig**.

And somewhere much later, when we finally arrive at Yoneda, we may discover that mathematics has been quietly waiting for us at the same intersection.
